# Solarpunk: Notes toward a manifesto

*Adam Flynn — September 8, 2014*

It's difficult to be a futurist under the age of thirty these days.

As we made our way through the educational systems of our respective countries, we encountered [WorldChanging](https://www.worldchanging.com/) and TED talks, carefully designed green consumerism, and nongovernmental organizations dedicated to sustainable development.

At the same time, however, we grew up surrounded by apocalyptic predictions expected to come true before we even reached retirement age; by the slow but relentless militarization of metropolitan police forces; and by the inability of the existing political order to confront the existential threat of climate change, which was still not being treated as an urgent matter.

Many of us feel that bringing children into a world like ours would be unethical.

We grew up under a shadow, and if we sometimes resemble fungi, that should be understood as recognition of our capacity to adapt.

**We are solarpunks because the only alternatives are denial or despair.**

The promises made by most singularitarians and transhumanists are individualistic and unsustainable. How many of them were conceived for a world in which energy is neither cheap nor abundant, let alone one facing shortages of rare-earth elements?

Solarpunk is about finding ways to make life more extraordinary for us, here and now, and, more importantly, for the generations that will come after us. In other words, it is about extending human existence at the level of the species rather than pursuing individual longevity alone.

Our future must involve reusing what we already possess and creating new things from it, rather than following the twentieth-century modernist approach of destroying everything and building something entirely different.

Our futurism is not nihilistic like cyberpunk, and it avoids the potentially quasi-reactionary tendencies of steampunk.

It is about **ingenuity, creativity, independence, and community.**

And yes, there is a *-punk* in the name, and not merely because the suffix has become fashionable.

Solarpunk has an oppositional dimension, but that opposition begins with **infrastructure as a form of resistance.**

We can already observe this in the difficulties utility companies face as rooftop solar installations expand dramatically.

"Dealing with infrastructure is a form of protection against losing one's self-determination," said Chokwe Lumumba, the late mayor of Jackson, Mississippi. And he was right.

There are certainly good reasons to maintain an integrated electrical grid, and we do not want to see it deteriorate. Nevertheless, one of the positive aspects of local resilience is that it places communities in a much stronger position to negotiate with those who might seek to interrupt their energy supply.

*We're looking at you, Detroit.*

![Solarpunk punk](https://hieroglyph.wpenginepowered.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/Solarpunk-punk1.png)

Solarpunk draws inspiration from Thomas Jefferson's ideal of the independent small farmer, Gandhi's concept of [*swadeshi*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swadeshi_movement) and the subsequent [Salt March](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_march), as well as countless other traditions of **innovative dissent**.

(Incidentally, both Gandhi and Jefferson were also inventors.)

The visual aesthetics of solarpunk are open and constantly evolving. At present, they combine the following elements:

- The lives of nineteenth-century sailors and pioneers during the age of sail and frontier settlement, but with many more bicycles.
- Creative reuse of existing infrastructure, sometimes in post-apocalyptic settings and sometimes in strangely transformed versions of the present.
- [*Jugaad*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jugaad)-style innovation, characterized by ingenious, improvised, and economical solutions developed in emerging economies.
- Sophisticated technological systems operating behind the scenes, producing simple and elegant solutions for people.

Naturally, the further we move into the future, the more ambitious we can become.

In the long term, solarpunk takes the images presented by progressive environmental blogs and develops them more broadly, deeply, and enduringly.

Imagine permaculture specialists thinking on the timescale required to build a cathedral.

Consider terraced irrigation systems that also function as fluidic computers.

Contemplate the life of an officer in a Department of Environmental Reclamation, responsible for managing a sparsely populated region of the American Southwest now devoted to solar energy collection and pumped energy storage.

Imagine so-called "smart cities" being abandoned in favor of **smart citizens.**

Over the past week, Tumblr has been buzzing about [this post](https://missolivialouise.tumblr.com/post/94374063675/heres-a-thing-ive-had-around-in-my-head-for-a), which envisioned a form of solarpunk inspired by *Art Nouveau* and Edwardian gardens.

The result is stunning and reminds me of the work of [Hayao Miyazaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayao_Miyazaki).

There is something enchanting about the way this aesthetic responds to dominant visions of an excessively smooth, clean, white, iPod-like modernist future.

**Solarpunk is a future with a human face and a little dirt behind its ears.**
